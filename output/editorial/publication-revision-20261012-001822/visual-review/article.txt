      Hamiltonian and Hamilton-connected thresholds for planar
                        recolouring graphs



                                                 Abstract
         For a graph G, let Rk (G) have the proper colourings of G with a labelled k-colour palette as
     vertices, with one-vertex recolourings as edges. We prove that Rk (G) is Hamilton-connected and
     vertex-pancyclic whenever G is nonempty and d-degenerate and k ≥ d + 3; this uniform palette
     bound is sharp for both properties. We determine the common Hamiltonian and Hamilton-
     connected thresholds as 7 for planar graphs, 5 for triangle-free and bipartite planar graphs, and 4
     for planar graphs of girth at least six and for forests. At four colours we also prove Hamiltonicity
     for planar graphs of girth at least five with at most seven distinct 5-cycles, and for those whose
     every block has maximum degree at most three. The latter condition allows unbounded degree
     at cutvertices. These four-colour proofs combine structural reductions with local restoration
     certificates supplied in a separate, reproducible supplement. For unrestricted planar graphs of
     girth at least five, both thresholds remain between 4 and 5.


1    Introduction
A cyclic listing of all proper colourings in which successive colourings differ at one vertex is a
colouring Gray code, or equivalently a Hamilton cycle in the recolouring graph. Prescribing the
first and last colourings asks for Hamilton-connectedness. Requiring a cycle of every length through
each colouring asks for vertex-pancyclicity. We study these stronger spanning and cyclic properties
for sparse graphs, with particular emphasis on sharp palette thresholds for planar classes.
     All graphs are finite, simple and undirected, and may be disconnected. A graph is nonempty
when it has at least one vertex. Put [k] = {1, . . . , k}. The vertices of Rk (G) are the proper
maps σ : V (G) → [k]; two are adjacent when they differ on exactly one vertex of G. Colours are
labelled, and a colouring need not use every colour. A cycle has at least three vertices. A graph is
Hamilton-connected if every two distinct vertices are joined by a spanning path, and vertex-pancyclic
if every vertex lies on a cycle of every length from three to the order of the graph.
     For a class C of nonempty graphs, define

         h(C) = min{q : Rk (G) is Hamiltonian for every G ∈ C and every k ≥ q},
        hc(C) = min{q : Rk (G) is Hamilton-connected for every G ∈ C and every k ≥ q},

when these sets are nonempty. These are uniform thresholds in the palette size; the proofs establish
every larger palette separately. A graph is d-degenerate if each nonempty subgraph has a vertex of
degree at most d. Write Dd for this class.
   Choo and MacGillivray [1, Theorem 3.4] proved that Rk (G) is Hamiltonian for k ≥ d + 3 when
G is d-degenerate. They also determined the uniform forest threshold and several individual tree
and cycle cases [1, Sections 5–6]. Our first result strengthens their general Hamiltonian conclusion
while keeping the same palette bound.

connected and vertex-pancyclic. Moreover, hc(Dd ) = d + 3, and d + 3 is also the least uniform palette
bound for vertex-pancyclicity on Dd .

graphs, Pg for the planar graphs of girth at least g, and F for the forests. All these classes contain

Theorem 1.2. The following uniform thresholds are exact:

                                                      h(P) = hc(P) = 7,

                                  h(P6 ) = hc(P6 ) = h(F) = hc(F ) = 4.

    The large-palette cases follow from degeneracy. The critical palettes require endpoint-preserving
restoration of separated low-degree vertices, and, in the triangle-free case, of an adjacent degree-three
pair. The lower bounds use frozen colourings and the three-colour recolouring graph of a short path.
The equality h(F) = 4 already follows from [1]; here it accompanies the Hamilton-connected forest
result.
    At four colours the girth-five case requires different local configurations. Let P5≤7 consist of the
graphs in P5 with at most seven distinct 5-cycles. Let S5 consist of those in P5 whose every block
has maximum degree at most three. Here blocks include bridges and isolated vertices as well as
maximal two-connected subgraphs.

Theorem 1.3. For every G ∈ P5≤7 ∪ S5 and every k ≥ 4, Rk (G) is Hamiltonian. Both classes have
uniform Hamiltonian threshold four, and their Hamilton-connected thresholds lie between four and
five.

1.1    Proof strategy and organization
Deleting vertices partitions the larger recolouring graph into fibres above colourings of the smaller
graph. At the degenerate bound these fibres are cliques of order at least three. We choose
bridge colours between consecutive fibres to prescribe endpoints or cycle lengths. Section 2 proves
Theorem 1.1, including the parity obstruction that makes its bound sharp. Section 3 develops the
product and independent-set lifts used in the planar proofs.
    Section 4 proves Theorem 1.2 by combining these lifts with planar degree counts. Section 5
proves Theorem 1.3. The few-cycle argument restores low-degree configurations. For the subcubic
and block-subcubic classes, we restore two separated pentagons. Their product fibres have at most
four components. Local cycles retain protected edges at each interface; a bounded-degree spanning
tree then joins all component cycles into one spanning cycle, for a bottom recolouring cycle of
arbitrary length.
    The supplement Finite certificates for Hamiltonian thresholds in planar recolouring graphs
contains the literal paths, cycles, pools and interface records. Its Appendices A–D correspond to the
adjacent-pair, path and pentagon steps. Section 7 specifies exactly what the accompanying verifiers
check. The finite data supply local lemmas; the inductions and restoration arguments in the article
give the conclusions for graphs of arbitrary order.

1.2    Notation
For a graph G, write NG (v) for the open neighbourhood, NG [v] = NG (v) ∪ {v}, and G − S for
deletion of a vertex set S. The Cartesian product X□Y joins (x, y) to (x′ , y ′ ) when exactly one
coordinate changes along an edge of its factor. A spanning path may be a singleton when its ends
coincide in an intermediate construction. Local certificates use zero-based palettes {0, . . . , k − 1},
obtained by relabelling [k]. Face lengths count edge-side incidences, so a bridge is counted twice.


2     The degenerate theorem
We begin with the general result that supplies all large-palette statements.

Lemma 2.1 (Cyclic list selection). Let m ≥ 3 and let L1 , . . . , Lm be finite sets of size at least two.
Unless m is odd and all Li are the same two-element set, there are xi ∈ Li with xi ̸= xi+1 cyclically.

Proof. If some Li has at least three elements, choose successively along the path obtained by deleting
that position, then choose the remaining element to avoid both end values. If all sets are binary and
two consecutive sets differ, start with a value in their symmetric difference and continue greedily. If
all are the same binary set, alternation is possible exactly when m is even.

Lemma 2.2 (One-vertex lift). Let H = G − v ̸= ∅ and suppose k ≥ dG (v) + 3. If Rk (H) has a
Hamilton cycle, then Rk (G) has a Hamilton cycle.

Proof. Write a Hamilton cycle of Rk (H) as f1 , . . . , fm , f1 and put Li = [k] \ fi (NG (v)). Then
|Li | ≥ 3 and |Li ∩ Li+1 | ≥ 2. Lemma 2.1 gives cyclic choices xi ∈ Li ∩ Li+1 with consecutive values
distinct. The extensions above fi form a clique on Li ; traverse that clique by a Hamilton path
from xi−1 to xi , and use the edge with v fixed at xi to cross to the next fibre. The exceptional
all-identical binary case is impossible because every proper colouring of H occurs on the base cycle
and a global colour permutation changes a neighbour of v to either binary colour. The fibre paths
therefore concatenate to a spanning cycle.

                           |L(f )| ≥ 3,       |L(f ) ∩ L(g)| ≥ |L(f )| − 1 ≥ 2.                     (2.1)

Theorem 2.4 (Degenerate Hamilton-connectedness). Let d ≥ 0 be an integer, let G be a nonempty
d-degenerate graph, and let k ≥ d + 3 be an integer. Then Rk (G) is Hamilton-connected.

Proof. Induct on n = |V (G)|. The case n = 1 is Kk . If G = Kn , then Rk (G) is the arrangement
graph Ak,n , whose vertices are ordered n-tuples of distinct colours. Since k ≥ n + 2, it is Hamilton-
connected by [3, Theorem 1].
    Otherwise choose a minimum-degree vertex v and a nonneighbour w ∈ V (H), where H = G − v.
Both exist because G is not complete, and dG (v) ≤ d. By induction, Y = Rk (H) is Hamilton-
connected. Use the clique fibres just defined.
    Path-lifting claim. Let f0 , . . . , ft be a base path, t ≥ 1, and set Si = L(fi ) ∩ L(fi+1 ). If some
Sj has size at least three, then the union of its fibres has a spanning path from f0 (a) to ft (b) for
arbitrary a ∈ L(f0 ) and b ∈ L(ft ). Indeed, Lemma 2.3 chooses xi ∈ Si with x0 ̸= a, consecutive
xi distinct, and xt−1 ̸= b. Traverse each clique between its prescribed entrance and exit, and join
consecutive traversals by fi (xi )fi+1 (xi ).
    This claim applies to every base path missing at most one vertex of Y . To see this, colour
permutations give all k colours at w among the states of Y . After removing at most one state, at
least k − 1 ≥ 2 colours still occur at w. A path through those states therefore contains a move at w.
This move leaves L(f ) unchanged, so its intersection has size at least three.
    Now prescribe distinct endpoints α(a) and β(b) in Rk (G). If α ̸= β, lift a Hamilton path in Y
from α to β by the claim. This covers every fibre.
    If α = β, then a ̸= b. Choose c ∈ L(α) \ {a, b}. A vertex of degree at most d in H has at least
three available colours. Recolour it to a colour different from its present colour and from c, obtaining

Proposition 2.7 (Optimal uniform palette bound). For every d ≥ 0, hc(Dd ) = d + 3. The same
uniform palette bound for vertex-pancyclicity cannot be reduced.
Proof. The upper bounds are the preceding theorems. For d ≥ 1, take G = Kd+1 and k = d + 2.

of d + 2 colours changes by a transposition at every recolouring. Thus Rd+2 (G) is bipartite by
permutation parity, with equal parts and even order (d + 2)! ≥ 6. A spanning path must have
endpoints in opposite parts, so the graph is not Hamilton-connected. It has no triangle, so it is
not vertex-pancyclic. For d = 0, the two-isolated-vertex graph has R2 (G) = C4 , which fails both
properties.


3    Endpoint lifting and restoration
A colouring of a deleted graph determines the available colours at the vertices to be restored. The
resulting extension graph is a fibre. In all the constructions below, assignments retain their actual
coordinate values: equal assignments in two different fibres are distinct vertices, and a cross edge
joins their two copies. In particular, a move of one vertex of the deleted graph may change several
available lists at once.
Lemma 3.1 (Products of complete graphs). Let s ≥ 1 and F = □sj=1 KAj , where every Aj is finite
and |Aj | ≥ 2. If all lists have size two, F has a Hamilton path between two prescribed vertices if
and only if those vertices lie in opposite bipartition classes. If at least one list has size at least three,
F is Hamilton-connected.
Proof. In the binary case F is the hypercube Qs , whose bipartition classes have equal size. The
endpoint condition is therefore necessary. For sufficiency, induct on s, starting with the single
edge Q1 . Choose a coordinate on which the prescribed endpoints differ and split along it. Their
projections into Qs−1 have the same parity. Choose a projected vertex of the opposite parity; use
the two induction paths to and from its copies, joined by the edge between those copies.
    For the second assertion, we first show that Y □Kr is Hamilton-connected whenever Y is
Hamilton-connected and |V (Y )| ≥ 3. Induct on r, starting with r = 1. If the two endpoints lie
in different copies of Y , order the copies starting and ending at those copies. Choose successive
bridge vertices in V (Y ), with the first different from the first endpoint projection, consecutive
bridges different, and the last different from the last endpoint projection. Such choices exist because
|V (Y )| ≥ 3: choose greedily, leaving the last bridge until the end, when it has at most two forbidden
values. Prescribed-end Hamilton paths inside the copies then join into a spanning path. If both
endpoints lie in one copy, take a Hamilton path between them there and choose an edge xy of it.
By induction the other r − 1 copies have a spanning path from a copy of x to a copy of y in the
same one of those copies. Insert that path in place of xy, using the two cross edges. Start with a
complete-graph factor of order at least three and add the remaining factors to obtain the claim.

be disjoint fibres arranged along a path. Join equal assignments in consecutive fibres. At every

                               |Ai,j \ Ai+1,j | ≤ 1,         |Ai+1,j \ Ai,j | ≤ 1.

If an internal fibre Fr , 1 ≤ r ≤ m − 2, has a factor of order at least three, then every prescribed
p ∈ F0 and q ∈ Fm−1 are joined by a path covering all vertices of all fibres exactly once.

Proof. Write
                                        Ji = □sj=1 KAi,j ∩Ai+1,j
for the graph of common assignments. The intersections are nonempty, and an unchanged coordinate
supplies an edge of Ji .
     Starting at p, record the reachable exit assignments at each interface. If a fibre has a reachable
entrance a, an interface edge has an admissible Hamilton-path endpoint: in a binary fibre choose
its endpoint of parity opposite to a, and in a nonbinary fibre choose an endpoint different from a.
Lemma 3.1 therefore shows that the reachable set never becomes empty.
    At Fr , its outgoing interface Jr still contains an edge after any one assignment is removed.
Indeed, if its large coordinate is unchanged, Jr contains a triangle. Otherwise that coordinate has
intersection size at least two, and a different unchanged coordinate supplies a K2 □K2 . Take a
reachable entrance a in Fr . Hamilton-connectedness of Fr makes every assignment in V (Jr ) \ {a}
reachable; this set contains an entire edge.
    An incoming reachable edge permits every outgoing assignment to be reached in the next fibre.
In a binary fibre the two endpoints of the edge have opposite parities, so one has the parity needed
for any specified outgoing assignment. In a nonbinary fibre they are distinct, so one differs from
that assignment. Thus the reachable exits contain an edge at every subsequent interface. At the
last fibre, one endpoint of the incoming edge can be joined to the specified q. Backtracking the
finite choices supplies one Hamilton path in each fibre; the actual cross edges concatenate them into
the required spanning path.

Lemma 3.3 (Hamilton-connected independent-set restoration). Let S be a nonempty independent
set, H = G − S ̸= ∅, and k ≥ 4. Suppose

                 dG (u) ≤ k − 2   (u ∈ S),      |NG (x) ∩ S| ≤ |S| − 1   (x ∈ V (H)).

Assume that Rk (H) is Hamilton-connected and that some proper k-colouring of H leaves at least
three colours available at a vertex of S. If α, β are proper k-colourings of G with α|H ̸= β|H , then
Rk (G) has an α–β Hamilton path.

Proof. Take a Hamilton path of Rk (H) between the two restriction colourings. For a base colouring
f , the fibre is exactly
                           □u∈S KAf (u) ,   Af (u) = [k] \ f (NG (u)).
Every list is at least binary. A move at an actual vertex x of H leaves the list of at least one
non-neighbour in S unchanged; every other list loses and gains at most one colour. These are the
transition conditions of Lemma 3.2, even when several lists change together.
     A colouring with a nonbinary fibre has at least k distinct colour permutations: fix a vertex of
the nonempty H and send its colour to each of the k labels. Every resulting fibre is nonbinary. The
base Hamilton path has only two endpoints, so one of these colourings is internal. Lemma 3.2 now
lifts the path with the specified, actual colours of α and β on S.

    For use of this lemma, first choose a vertex w on which two prescribed colourings differ, and
then choose S with w ∈ / S. This ensures different restriction colourings; the lemma does not claim
to handle arbitrary endpoints in the same fibre.

Lemma 3.4 (Cyclic product-fibre lift). Let m ≥ 3 disjoint fibres Fi = □sj=1 KAi,j , with s ≥ 1
and |Ai,j | ≥ 2, be cyclically ordered. Join equal assignments in consecutive fibres and define
Ji = □j KAi,j ∩Ai+1,j , with indices modulo m. Suppose every Ji contains an edge, F0 has a factor

Proof. Choose an edge ei in every remaining interface, and choose z0 ∈ V (e0 ) \ V (em−1 ). For

send a common member to the other target member when there is exactly one common member,
and use either bijection when the sets are disjoint.
    Put zi = ϕi (zi−1 ). Traverse Fi from zi−1 to zi for 1 ≤ i < m. Since zm−1 ∈ V (em−1 ), zm−1 ̸= z0 ,
so the nonbinary F0 has a Hamilton path from zm−1 to z0 . Join the fibre paths by the cross edges
at the assignments zi . They cover every fibre once and form one cycle.

Lemma 3.5 (Independent-set restoration). Let S be a nonempty independent set, H = G − S ̸= ∅,
and k ≥ 4. Suppose

                  dG (u) ≤ k − 2    (u ∈ S),       |NG (x) ∩ S| ≤ |S| − 1    (x ∈ V (H)).

If Rk (H) is Hamiltonian and some proper k-colouring of H leaves at least three colours available at
a vertex of S, then Rk (G) is Hamiltonian.

Proof. Take a Hamilton cycle of the base and use the same product fibres as in Lemma 3.3. Every
interface contains an edge. Index a nonbinary fibre as F0 and choose a large coordinate in it. At
either adjacent interface, that coordinate supplies a triangle if unchanged, or a K2 □K2 together
with a different unchanged coordinate if it changes. Thus both adjacent interfaces have at least two
edges, and distinct edges e0 , em−1 can be chosen. Apply Lemma 3.4.

Lemma 3.6 (Single-vertex Hamilton-connected lift). Let H = G − v ̸= ∅, k ≥ 4, and k ≥ dG (v) + 3.
Assume that v has a non-neighbour in G, Rk (H) is Hamilton-connected, and δ(Rk (H)) > dG (v).
Then Rk (G) is Hamilton-connected.

Proof. For a base colouring f , write Af = [k] \ f (NG (v)). The fibre is the clique KAf , |Af | ≥ 3,
and adjacent base fibres have intersections of size at least two.
     Let w be a non-neighbour of v. Any base path covering all states, or all but one state, has a
step changing w. Indeed, global colour permutations show that w takes all k colours among the
base states; omitting one state leaves at least k − 1 ≥ 3 colours. At a step changing w, the available
list for v is unchanged, so its intersection has size at least three. Consequently such a base path can
be lifted with arbitrary prescribed first and last fibre colours. Indeed, Lemma 2.3 supplies bridge
colours in the successive intersections, with distinct entrance and exit colours in each clique and
with the prescribed endpoint colours avoided at the two ends. Traverse each clique between these
colours and join the paths by the corresponding cross edges.
     If the prescribed states lie in different fibres, lift the base Hamilton path between their restrictions.
Suppose instead that both lie over f , with distinct colours a, b. A Hamilton-connected graph with
at least three vertices has a Hamilton cycle through every edge: close a Hamilton path between the
ends of the edge by that edge. Here the base has at least k states.
     Choose any c ∈ Af \ {a, b}. At most dG (v) base moves from f make c unavailable: for each
neighbour of v, only its move to colour c can do so. The state-degree assumption therefore gives

                                                      8
a base edge f g preserving c. Take a base Hamilton cycle C containing f g, and let h be the other
neighbour of f on C. Choose q ∈ (Af ∩ Ah ) \ {c}, which is possible because the intersection has
size at least two. If q = a, interchange the endpoints and reverse the final path, so we may assume
q ̸= a.
    As in the same-fibre case of Theorem 2.4, split the root clique into disjoint paths a ⇝ c and
q ⇝ b covering it; the second is a singleton when q = b, and otherwise is the edge qb. Lift C − f
through all other fibres with endpoint colours c, q. Joining this lifted path to the two root paths by
the cross edges at c, q gives the prescribed spanning path.


    A Hamilton-connected graph of order at least four has minimum degree at least three. A vertex
of degree two would, with its neighbours chosen as the two endpoints, force a spanning path to have
only three vertices; degrees zero and one are excluded as well. Every nonempty colourable H has at
least k states in Rk (H), by colour permutation. These observations will supply the state-degree
condition in the low-degree inductions.


4     Hamilton-connected planar classes
4.1   All planar graphs
We first establish the marked structural statement needed to retain a coordinate on which the
prescribed endpoint colourings differ.
Lemma 4.1 (Separated degree-five vertices with a marked vertex). If G is planar and δ(G) ≥ 5,
then, for any w ∈ V (G), there are two degree-five vertices different from w whose distance is at least

5.5   The complete path-lifting induction
Lemma 5.10 ((2, 3, 2)-path lifting). Suppose the three external neighbours of a degree-(2, 3, 2) path
are distinct and outside it. If the reconfiguration graph of the deleted graph is Hamiltonian at four
colours, then so is R4 (G).

Proof. Fix an arbitrary bottom Hamilton cycle. There must be a good vertex on it. Otherwise every
boundary is constant; consecutive constant triples can only be equal, as at most one coordinate
changes. The external colour would therefore be fixed throughout the cycle. A global colour
permutation of a proper colouring supplies another proper colouring with a different external colour,
a contradiction to Hamilton coverage. Thus even the startup uses only Hamiltonicity.
    Every good bottom vertex has at most one bad neighbour in the entire bottom reconfiguration
graph: a two-colour boundary has exactly one possible coordinate change making it constant, and
an all-distinct boundary has none. The full neighbouring colouring, if proper, is uniquely determined
by that change. Hence no good vertex on the Hamilton cycle can lie between two bad vertices. In
particular there is an edge joining two good vertices.
    Cut such an edge. In the resulting bottom Hamilton path, group each maximal bad run together
with its two neighbouring good vertices; make each remaining good vertex a singleton block. These


                                                    18
blocks are disjoint and partition the path. A bad run has constant boundary, and its two neighbours
are distinct. If the run has length one their boundaries are different: otherwise both neighbours are
obtained from the intervening colouring by the same one-vertex recolouring and would be the same
bottom vertex.
    For each labelled good boundary fix one allowed cycle once and for all. Consecutive blocks meet
at a good–good boundary transition. If the boundary changes, Lemma 5.8 provides two common
safe edges. If it stays unchanged, the fixed cycles coincide and have at least 14 − 2 safe edges. Select
one connecting edge for each interblock interface in path order. If a singleton block already has an
incoming selected edge, choose a different outgoing one, which is possible from the two candidates.
In a nonsingleton block the two selected edges are in its distinct end fibres, so cannot conflict.
    Use Lemma 5.9 to construct each nonsingleton block cycle preserving its selected end edges;
singleton blocks already have their fixed cycles. Splice the resulting disjoint block cycles along the
selected interfaces. Each step joins two cycles into one, and every later selected edge is still present.
The one-block case needs no final splice. All transitions are legal: a local step changes one path
vertex, and a matching cross-edge performs its actual bottom recolouring while holding the local
colouring fixed. Finally the fibres are disjoint and exhaust all proper colourings. Thus the resulting
single cycle is Hamiltonian, including its closing edge.

5.8   An arbitrary bottom cycle
Lemma 5.16. Suppose G has girth at least five and contains two vertex-disjoint 5-cycles with no
edge between them. Each cycle has vertices of degree three except for at most one vertex of degree
two. Let H be obtained by deleting both cycles. If R4 (H) is Hamiltonian, so is R4 (G). Neither
planarity nor three-colourability of H is assumed here.
Proof. Each degree-three cycle vertex has one exterior neighbour; a degree-two vertex gives a full
list. An exterior vertex meets a given 5-cycle at most once, since two attachments would create a 3-
or 4-cycle. Thus a bottom recolouring changes at most one list in each factor. The two factors may
change simultaneously. All exterior neighbours remain in H, which is nonempty.
     Along a bottom Hamilton cycle, every ordinary pentagon has a nonconstant boundary somewhere.
Otherwise consecutive constant five-tuples, differing in at most one entry, must be equal. Its entire
boundary would stay fixed along the bottom cycle. A global colour permutation changing that
colour supplies another proper colouring of H which the Hamilton cycle must visit, a contradiction.
These nonconstant layers need not coincide for the two factors.
     Make a component graph whose vertices are all product components in all bottom layers, joining
components in consecutive layers when they have a common local state. There are at most four
components per layer and every component-graph degree is at most eight. This graph is connected.
Indeed, on each constant run the two 15-cycle components can be labelled consistently by a binary
bit: consecutive constant boundaries are equal. A nonconstant layer forgets, or resets, that bit. A
starred factor resets it at every layer. Take four bit-labelled copies of the bottom cycle and allow a
flip of each bit at its reset layers. This graph is connected because each bit has a reset somewhere.
Contract the reset edges within each layer. The result is exactly the component graph: intersections
between nonconstant components are nonempty, each constant component meets its nonconstant
neighbour, and an unchanged constant component meets precisely itself.
     Choose a spanning tree of the component graph. Give the root one product cycle from Lemma 5.14
and record its two restoration maps. For a child, apply the inverse parent maps to the target

common state. This makes the source a standard type for the protected-edge table, so the two

5.10    Induction, checks and the remaining frontier
Proof of Theorem 5.11. Induct on the number of vertices, allowing disconnected graphs. The one-
vertex case is R4 (G) = K4 . A vertex of degree at most one is handled by Lemma 2.2. If there are
two degree-two vertices, adjacent ones use Lemma 5.5. Ones at distance two use Lemma 5.10, unless
their middle vertex has degree two, when an adjacent pair already applies. Ones at distance at least
three use Lemma 5.6, whose spare-colour witness uses Grötzsch’s theorem [5]; the new pentagon lift
does not. Pairs in different components have disjoint neighbourhoods and are covered by the same
separated-pair argument.
    The remaining graph has minimum degree at least two and at most one degree-two vertex.
Apply Lemma 5.17 inside any component. Delete its two separated pentagons. The remaining
graph is nonempty and belongs to the same class, so induction gives its four-colour Hamilton cycle.
Lemma 5.16 restores both pentagons. This proves the assertion at four colours. For every k ≥ 5
use the triangle-free planar theorem proved earlier, with larger colours separately supplied there by
degeneracy. The frozen 012012 colouring of C6 gives the Hamiltonian threshold lower bound four.
No monotonicity in k is presumed.

5.11    Graphs with subcubic blocks
Theorem 5.18 (Subcubic blocks). Let G be a nonempty finite simple planar graph of girth at least
five. If every block B satisfies ∆(B) ≤ 3, then Rk (G) is Hamiltonian for every k ≥ 4. Cutvertices
may have arbitrarily large degree in G. The uniform Hamiltonian threshold of this class is exactly
four. Its Hamilton-connected threshold lies between four and five.

Proof. Treat bridges as two-vertex blocks and isolated vertices as singleton blocks. The class is
closed under vertex deletion: a nontrivial two-connected block of a subgraph is a two-connected

subgraph of G, hence is contained in one original block, with no larger internal degrees. Planarity
and girth are also preserved.

another factor cycle, and merge successive copies using two alternating, distinct internal edges. This
also handles disconnected original graphs. Choose a smallest four-colour counterexample, which
may therefore be assumed connected. The singleton base is K4 , and the single-vertex lift excludes
vertices of degree at most one. If there is only one block, the previous subcubic girth-five theorem

The lower bounds used above are frozen colourings, not claims about a particular embedding.

        disconnected. Hence the planar threshold is at least seven.

Proof. For (i)–(iii), every vertex sees all colours other than its own, so the displayed colouring is
