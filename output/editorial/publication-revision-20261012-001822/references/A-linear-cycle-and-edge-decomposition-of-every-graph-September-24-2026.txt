A linear cycle-and-edge decomposition of every graph
OpenAI

Abstract
We prove that every finite simple undirected graph on n vertices has an edge partition
into at most Cn simple cycles and single edges, for an absolute constant C. This resolves the
Erdős–Gallai cycle decomposition conjecture positively.

Introduction

How efficiently can the edges of a graph be partitioned into cycles? Single edges must also be
allowed: a forest contains no cycle. The Erdős–Gallai cycle decomposition conjecture asserts that
this necessary allowance suffices for a universal linear bound. Here and throughout, a cycle is simple
and has length at least three.
Theorem 1.1. There is an absolute constant C > 0 such that, for every finite simple undirected
graph G with n vertices, E(G) has a partition into at most Cn parts, each of which is the edge set
of a cycle or consists of a single edge.
The parts in this theorem may share vertices. They share no edges, and every edge of G occurs
in exactly one part. Isolated vertices need not be covered, and the empty partition is permitted
when E(G) = ∅.
The conjecture appears in the 1966 paper of Erdős, Goodman and Pósa [6, Section 5], which
explicitly distinguishes unrestricted covers from edge-disjoint covers and records an O(n log n) upper
bound. Conlon, Fox and Sudakov [5] improved the general bound to O(n log log n) in 2014. Bucić
and Montgomery [4] subsequently proved the bound O(n log∗ n); their paper appeared in journal
form in 2024. Here log∗ n is the number of logarithm iterations needed to reach at most one.
Theorem 1.1 establishes the conjectured linear bound. Its order is optimal, since a tree on n vertices
requires n − 1 single-edge parts. More sharply, complete bipartite examples require (3/2 − o(1))n
parts; see [4, Section 6] for this lower bound and its history.
Linear bounds were already known for important classes. Conlon, Fox and Sudakov [5, Theorems 1.3 and 1.4] proved them with high probability for binomial random graphs, and deterministically for graphs of linear minimum degree. Korándi, Krivelevich and Sudakov [8] obtained
asymptotically sharp counts over a broad range of random-graph probabilities. For every fixed
α, δ > 0, Girão, Granet, Kühn and Osthus [7, Theorem 1.10(iii)] proved that sufficiently large
graphs of minimum degree at least αn admit at most (3/2 + δ)n cycles and edges. Akbari, Aloni,
Beikmohammadi and Clow [2, Theorem 1.3] proved the bound n − 1 for graphs of positive order
and maximum degree at most four. The general problem allows both sparse regions and vertices of
widely differing degrees.
The edge-disjoint requirement is essential to the problem. If cycles and edges may overlap,
Pyber [11] proved that n − 1 parts suffice to cover every graph of positive order. Akbari, Aloni,

Theorem 1.1 also yields two cycle-only consequences. Here a graph is Eulerian if all its degrees
are even; connectivity is not required.
Corollary 1.2. Every finite simple undirected Eulerian graph on n vertices has an edge partition
into at most Cn cycles, with C from Theorem 1.1.
For every fixed δ, p > 0, there exist ε > 0 and an integer n0 ≥ 1 such that every finite simple
undirected Eulerian graph G on n ≥ n0 vertices has an edge partition into at most ∆(G)/2 + δn
cycles, provided that every partition V (G) = A ∪˙ B with |A|, |B| ≥ εn satisfies
eG (A, B) ≥ p|A||B|.
Here ∆(G) denotes the maximum degree, and eG (A, B) is the number of edges joining A to B.
Any edge partition of G into cycles needs at least ∆(G)/2 cycles, since a cycle uses at most
two edges incident with a given vertex. The second assertion attains this lower bound up to an
arbitrarily small linear error under the stated large-cut condition.
Proof. For the first claim, let the partition from Theorem 1.1 have r cycle parts and s single-edge
parts, with r + s ≤ Cn. The graph formed by the latter edges has even degrees, since deleting
cycles preserves degree parity. Repeatedly removing a cycle preserves this property and ends with
no edges: every even graph with an edge contains a cycle. This uses at most s cycles, for a total
of at most r + s ≤ Cn. The equivalence of this O(n) Eulerian cycle bound with the Erdős–Gallai
conjecture is recorded in [7, Section 1.1].
The partition-cut condition in the second claim is exactly the weakly-(ε, p)-quasirandom condition
of [7, Section 1.2]. Their Proposition 6.3 proves that Conjecture 1.14, the stated bound with these
quantifiers, is equivalent to their Erdős–Gallai Conjecture 1.4, supplied by Theorem 1.1.
Our argument builds on the expansion, hypergraph matching and path-closing methods of Bucić
and Montgomery [4], with earlier reservoir and sparse-cut methods of Conlon, Fox and Sudakov [5].
We use Lovász’s path-and-cycle decomposition theorem [10] and the Aharoni–Haxell matching
theorem [1], in the precise forms stated below. The additional mechanism is an induction that saves
vertices across different scales while reserving enough edges to lift cycles from the smaller graphs. A
decomposition that repeatedly pays a cost proportional to the full graph order can accumulate a
factor from the number of scales. Here the work over a suitable prefix is charged to one expanding
layer, and pair identifications save a fixed fraction of that layer’s order. The savings pay for the
work outside the recursive calls.

We describe the nonterminal case; the terminal cases are treated separately in Section 7. After
the fixed gap in scales, the unprocessed edges are sparse and live on very small descendant boxes.
We gather those boxes into batches. Within each earlier box Y , all but O(|Y | / log D) vertices have
small degree in the union of that box’s batch graphs. We pair these vertices in sufficiently large
intersections between a batch and a selected expanding piece, and identify each pair. Edges between
a folding set and vertices having too many neighbors in that set are first partitioned into paths;
reserved routes close the trimmed remnants into cycles at linear cost. The remaining graph can
then be simplified after pair identification by setting aside only linearly many edges as single-edge
parts (Theorems 6.1 and 6.2).
The quotient graphs have fewer vertices, so induction partitions their edges into cycles and single
edges. Each quotient edge retains one original representative. At a paired vertex, the representatives
of a quotient cycle may enter through one member of the pair and leave through the other. We join
these two members by a path in the reserved expanding piece. The routing lemma chooses these
paths edge-disjointly, with interiors that avoid the representative endpoints and are pairwise disjoint
within each cycle (Theorem 3.1). Consequently each quotient cycle lifts to one simple cycle. The
degree bound is imposed on the union of the batch graphs within each earlier box, so the routing
load does not grow with the number of batches.

Conventions and preliminary results

2.1

Graphs, partitions and scales

Unless explicitly stated otherwise, graphs are finite, simple and undirected. A path is simple and
has at least one edge. The order of a graph is its number of vertices, including any isolated vertices
that have been retained. Temporary quotients formed by identifying vertices may have loops or
parallel edges; these are removed before any decomposition theorem is applied.
